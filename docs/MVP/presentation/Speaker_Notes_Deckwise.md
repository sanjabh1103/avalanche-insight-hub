# Speaker Notes For Five-Deck MVP Scientist Discussion Pack

Updated: May 14, 2026

Use these notes as presenter support after the decks are generated. They are intentionally simple, short, and beginner-friendly. Keep the same proof boundaries as the slide decks: current state, technical evidence, candidate/gated paths, and future strategy.

Numbering note: slide 1 is the agenda slide in each deck. The content-slide notes below therefore start at slide 2 and continue through slide 16.

## Speaker Rules

- Use full forms the first time a short form appears.
- Explain technical terms with a real-life comparison.
- Do not present the platform as an authority-grade public warning product.
- Do not say the scientist validation program has been completed.
- Do not say Multi-Time-Scale Long Short-Term Memory is the active public scorer.
- Do not say Synthetic Aperture Radar is promoted into active public scoring.
- Do not say TreeSHAP is active-run proven unless the artifact explicitly proves it.

## Deck 1: Current MVP Proof And Customer Value

### Slide 2: Avalanche Insight Hub

**Actual deck heading:** `D1-1 - Avalanche Insight Hub`

**Simple talk track:** Set the meeting contract: this is a governed avalanche decision-support MVP, not a finished authority product.

**Technical explanation:** Governed decision support means the system makes evidence visible, keeps uncertainty visible, and separates live proof from future strategy.

**Real-life example:** Like a hospital triage board: it organizes evidence quickly, but the specialist still owns the final clinical judgment.

**What not to overclaim:** Do not imply the platform replaces avalanche centers or completes scientist validation.

**Website callback:** Use the public URL after this framing if the audience needs immediate proof that the product exists.

### Slide 3: Avalanche Forecasting Remains Hard

**Actual deck heading:** `D1-2 - Avalanche Forecasting Remains Hard`

**Simple talk track:** Explain why this problem deserves careful architecture: observations are sparse, weak layers are hidden, and avalanche days are rare.

**Technical explanation:** Sparse data means the model sees incomplete evidence; rare-event imbalance means high accuracy can still miss the dangerous days.

**Real-life example:** A road-safety model can look accurate if most days are safe, but the value is in catching the few days when bridges ice over.

**What not to overclaim:** Do not oversell broad weather data as a substitute for snowpack validation.

### Slide 4: The Client’s Research Lineage

**Actual deck heading:** `D1-3 - The Client’s Research Lineage`

**Simple talk track:** Show that the MVP is grounded in years of avalanche modeling research, not a generic map demo.

**Technical explanation:** Artificial Neural Networks, calibration, feature selection, and imbalance handling are prior research threads that shaped this MVP.

**Real-life example:** This is like improving a long-running engineering design rather than inventing the first bridge.

**What not to overclaim:** Do not claim every research method is active in the public scorer today.

### Slide 5: What Earlier Research Solved, And What It Did Not

**Actual deck heading:** `D1-4 - What Earlier Research Solved, And What It Did Not`

**Simple talk track:** Separate solved engineering patterns from the hard remaining science: weak-layer validation, regional transfer, and remote sensing.

**Technical explanation:** Feature discipline reduces noisy inputs; validation checks whether the result holds outside the development dataset.

**Real-life example:** A weather app may use good sensors, but mountain guides still verify whether a local slope behaves differently.

**What not to overclaim:** Do not imply the current MVP solves weak-layer science completely.
